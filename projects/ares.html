Ares

Sumo robotics traces its roots back to the 20th century, inspired by the traditional sport of Japan, Sumo wrestling. It has now evolved to what is commonly known as a fighting – wrestling- robot, the word itself means “to compete” or “to strike one another”. Ancient Japanese texts describe muscular men engaging in bare-handed struggles with few rules in place. What started as a raw primal contest and a form of religious ritual designed to ensure bountiful harvests and divine favour has now gradually been refined and changed into many forms.

The Ares project refers to the category of Japanese robot wrestling, also known as Sumo. This category branches into several subcategories, such as Mini or Mega Sumo, depending on the weight and dimensions of the robot's frame. The goal of these robots is to push the opponent out of the ring, avoid them, search for them, and ensure they do not exit the ring when they reach its boundaries. It is one of the most popular robot categories, appearing in every competition, with participation numbers being literally huge. Named after the Greek god of war, Aris who is symbolizing strength, strategy, and combat, our robot is designed to compete fiercely and strategically in the Sumo ring.

Sumo robotics may look simple at first glance but behind every fast push and sharp turn lies careful engineering and smart programming. Most common materials used in building such robots are polymers and metal alloys, due to the weight ‘gain’ benefit they provide. Key required properties are high impact resistance, good machinability, toughness, wear resistance and medium to high tensile strength. Also, high density, good strength to weight ratio, high durability and endurance strength are very important. The sumo robot is programmed with three primarily objectives in mind. Manoeuvring or moving inside the dohyo, detection of opponent throughout the match and detection of the edge of the ring (white lines in contrast of the black center). To achieve each one of those goals in programming we use algorithms such as PID and Fuzzy logic, which help the robot correct its movement, maintain stability and allow it to react smoothly yet quickly to any detected changes. Detection is achieved by two groups of sensors. Those on the underside which help with the edge detection as they monitor the surface colour to identify the dohyo’s edge and those on the upper side, which track the opponent’s position during the match. Movement is achieved through the motor drivers that respond to sensor input and trigger predetermined (programmed) behaviours such us searching, attacking, turning and stopping. All these components connect through the robot’s electronic circuit, the central system that enables every separate part to function together.
